# Cartworth social: posting rules and legal risk

**Prepared:** 23 September 2026, for Nicholas Soderstrom (sole proprietor, Illinois).
**Covers:** the Cartworth brand accounts that post grocery price comparisons and trends, the owner's personal posts about Cartworth, and any creator Cartworth pays or gives Pro to.
**Read with:** `SOCIAL_MEDIA_BRIEF.md` §9 (this document tightens several of its rules), `APP_STORE_CHECKLIST.md` §13 (third-party data), `APP_STORE_LISTING.md` §11 (screenshot rules).

> **This is not legal advice.** I'm not a lawyer, and nothing here is an opinion on how the law applies to Cartworth. This document does three things. It sets posting rules, which are low-risk defaults you can adopt today. It summarizes what the statutes, regulators, courts and platforms say, with sources. And it lists the questions to take to a lawyer, ideally an Illinois advertising and IP lawyer, **before the account names a retailer in a post.** Where I couldn't read a source, I say so and treat what it says as unknown.

---

## The short version

1. **Truthful comparisons that name retailers are lawful, and the FTC encourages them.** But you need evidence for every claim a reasonable viewer takes away, including implied ones, and you need it *before* you post. A named retailer can sue under the Lanham Act, bring an Illinois deceptive-trade-practices claim, or file a challenge with the National Advertising Division (NAD).
2. **The biggest risk isn't a wrong number. It's a right number framed wrong.** Examples: an Instacart listing set against a shelf price, a store brand against a national brand, a sale against a regular price, one item presented as "this store is cheaper", or 6 sampled states presented as "America".
3. **Store names in plain text are fine. Logos, flyer art and product photos taken from sources are not.** In American Airlines v. Skiplagged (2024), the jury found the site's use of the airline's marks was nominative fair use, but awarded $9.4M for copying its logo.
4. **Publishing data collected from Instacart, Flipp and retailer sites is an open legal question.** Their terms ban scraping, and some specifically ban republishing. One court barred a fare scraper from publishing the airline's fares. Until a lawyer weighs in, any post that names a retailer should use first-hand data wherever possible: your own receipts and your own shelf observations.
5. **Correct a wrong post publicly within 24 hours.** Use the same format and at least the same prominence as the error, and never delete it silently.

## Part 1. Posting rules

These apply to every post, story, video, carousel, reply, pinned item, ad and creator post. A post that breaks one doesn't go up. Anything marked **(new)** is stricter than the current brief.

### A. Every price

1. **Evidence first. No evidence file, no post.** Before publishing, save a record for each price shown. It must include:
   - the exact product name, brand, size and UPC if available
   - the store name, store number or address, and ZIP
   - the basis (see rule 2)
   - capture date and time, and the source (receipt, shelf photo, or the page or app it came from)
   - sale or regular, and the sale end date
   - any conditions (loyalty card, digital coupon, must-buy-N, limit)
   - the arithmetic for any unit price or percentage

   Keep these records for at least 3 years, or as long as a lawyer tells you to. They're your substantiation file (see 3.1).
2. **Put the basis on every price, in words, on the image itself** and not only in the caption. Use exactly these labels:
   - "shelf price (receipt)" or "shelf price (shelf tag)": you saw it in the store
   - "Instacart listing": the store's Instacart price, which can differ from in-store
   - "chain-wide online listing": not the price at any particular store
   - "weekly ad, valid [dates]"

   Never say just "shelf" for a price Cartworth *inferred* from Instacart metadata. See rule 9 and finding 3.3.
3. **Put the date and place on the image itself,** for example "Prices checked Tue 23 Sep 2026, Chicago 60618". Screenshots travel without captions, so the label has to travel with the picture.
4. **Show the package price and size next to any unit price,** for example "$4.29 for 18 oz = $3.81/lb", so anyone can check the maths.
5. **Never post an estimated or flagged price.** That includes anything Cartworth marks `est`, anything sold by the each with an assumed weight, anything with a "price looks wrong" or off-scale flag, and anything the matcher picked that you haven't eyeballed.
6. **The cheapest number in the post gets a second check** (new): a receipt, a shelf tag, or a second capture on a different day. A price that's wrongly too low is the error that wins a comparison.
7. **Say whether it's a sale price, and when the sale ends.** Never set one store's sale price against another store's regular price unless the post says exactly that, on the image.
8. **Leave conditional prices out of comparisons.** That means loyalty-card, digital-coupon and multi-buy prices. If a post is *about* the condition, name it on the image ("with store card", "digital coupon, must clip").
9. **Before a post calls a price a shelf price, confirm it in person** (new). Right now Cartworth labels some Instacart prices "shelf" because the store says "No markups" or is listed as an "in-store" shop. That label is an inference, not an observation (finding 3.3). Until a receipt confirms it for that store, call it an "Instacart listing (store says no markups)".
10. **Re-check each named price within 24 hours before posting** (new). If it changed, re-capture it or don't post. Stay within the politeness rules: at least 700ms between requests to a host, never parallel requests to one host, and stop on a 429.
11. **Give comparisons that name a retailer a 7-day life** (new; the brief allows 14 days). After 7 days:
    - don't repost, re-share, boost, or recut them into a new video without re-checking the prices
    - never pin a price comparison, although you can pin corrections
    - check the prices again before every paid boost

### B. Every comparison

12. **Compare on the same basis only:** shelf against shelf, Instacart against Instacart, weekly ad against weekly ad. **Never compare across bases, even with a disclosure** (new). A disclosure can qualify a claim but can't cure one that's false (FTC; finding 3.3).
13. **Compare the same product,** or state the match on the image. Match store brand with store brand, name brand with name brand, and organic with organic. Never set one store's store brand against another's name brand without saying so. That mismatch is what sank Aldi's "save up to 50%" ads at NAD.
14. **Keep the scope in the words:** this item, these stores, this date. One item supports one-item claims only. Never go from item data to "Store A is cheaper", "Store A is the cheap store", or "shop at A".
15. **Show both prices, not just a percentage.** Work the percentage from the higher price ("A is 30% less than B" means A = 0.70 × B), say which way it runs, and round toward the smaller difference.
16. **Compare stores a real shopper would choose between.** Say the city or distance. Never set a Peoria store against a San Francisco store as if they were alternatives.
17. **Only use superlatives inside a stated scope.** "Lowest of the 9 stores we checked near 60618" is fine. "Cheapest in Chicago", "cheapest in America", "always" and "the lowest price" are banned.
18. **Name the sample on screen for aggregate posts** (new): "6 states, 12 ZIPs, 44 stores, lowest price found at the sampled stores in each state's largest metros. Not a statewide average." Don't publish state or national rankings while the pipeline rates an item's confidence as `low`, and in the current capture every item is `low`. The "Cheapest State" series is parked until coverage supports it (finding 3.1).
19. **If a post implies a pattern, disclose how many items you checked** and the win/loss count ("Aldi was lower on 6 of 9 items we checked"). Never pick out the one item that fits and imply it's typical.
20. **Only make trend claims** ("up 12% since August") on the same stores, the same products and the same basis at both dates, with both dates stated.
21. **Get a lawyer to review basket posts before they run** (new). That means any "full Thanksgiving dinner at each store" or "same cart at 3 stores" post that names retailers. Basket claims are what NAD reviewed in the Aldi case.

### C. Words

22. **Report numbers, not motives.** Don't say why a price differs ("because they mark it up"), and never say "ripping you off", "scam", "lying", "overcharging", "price gouging" or "they don't want you to know". Price gouging is a legal term in many states, and saying a named company broke the law is a defamation risk. TikTok's ad policy also bans comparisons that accuse a brand of serious violations.
23. **Don't say "Instacart marks up" or "Instacart charges more".** Instacart says the *retailer* sets its Instacart prices. The accurate phrase is "[Store]'s Instacart listing".
24. **Never promise savings.** No "save $X a week" and no "cut your bill". This rule is kept from the brief.
25. **Don't imply the data is official.** No "official", "verified by [store]", "from [store]'s system" or "partner".

### D. Names, logos and images

26. **Use retailer names in plain text only,** and only as much as you need to say which store it is. Never use:
    - logos
    - wordmarks set in the retailer's typeface
    - brand colours used to mark a store
    - mascots, taglines, or promotion trademarks used as design (for example "Rollback")
    - storefront signage as the subject of an image
27. **Never use retailer names in the account's handle, name, series titles or recurring hashtags** (new). Examples of what's banned: "Aldi Watch" and "#JewelDeals". That's using their mark as your brand. Only tag a retailer's name on a post that's actually about that retailer's prices.
28. **Never reuse images from Instacart, Flipp or retailer sites** (new). That covers product photos, flyer clippings, ad scraps and weekly ad pages, even cropped or blurred into the background. In **screen recordings of the app**, pick results whose thumbnails are plain product cut-outs, or blur the thumbnails. The flyer-art rule in `APP_STORE_LISTING.md` §11 applies to every frame.
29. **Photograph products yourself.** Use a neutral background and products you bought. Prefer text cards and charts to product hero shots. Store-brand packaging (Great Value, Friendly Farms, Kirkland, 365) *is* the retailer's trademark and trade dress, so don't make it the centrepiece.
30. **Don't film inside stores by default.** If you ever do:
    - get a manager's permission first
    - leave as soon as you're asked
    - keep shoppers and staff unidentifiable (Illinois Right of Publicity Act)
    - record no audio of other people's conversations (Illinois eavesdropping law)

    Photograph shelf tags only with permission.
31. **Put a disclaimer in every bio, on the pinned "about" post and on the methodology page:** "Independent. Not affiliated with or endorsed by any store." Add it to the image on any post that names a store.
32. **Credit OpenStreetMap on any map built from its data:** "© OpenStreetMap contributors".

### E. Sources and data

33. **Cartworth's data posts must link to a public methodology page on cartworth.app** (new). The page explains:
    - what each basis means, and that Instacart listings can differ from in-store prices
    - the sample
    - what's excluded (loyalty and coupon prices)
    - how products are matched
    - the corrections policy and log
34. **Tie posts that name a retailer and compare prices to first-hand data by default** (new): your receipts, shelf tags you photographed with permission, or the retailer's own weekly ad restated as text with its dates. This is the strongest evidence you can have, and it doesn't depend on any site's terms. Comparisons built on Instacart or Flipp data that name a retailer wait for a lawyer's view (finding 3.4).
35. **Never post bulk data** (new): no downloadable datasets, big price tables or "every price at every store" images. Show a handful of facts per post.
36. **Don't publish a product a matcher chose until a person has checked it's the right comparison.** In the 22 Sep capture, Cartworth's pick for Jewel-Osco whole milk was a $6.99 name-brand gallon, while a $3.99 "Jewel Milk" gallon was also listed (finding 3.1).

### F. Disclosure

37. **The owner, family, friends and anyone paid by Cartworth must disclose their connection whenever they mention Cartworth off the brand account.** That covers Reddit, Facebook groups, comments and reviews. Put it at the start of the post ("I make Cartworth"), not the end, and not only in a profile.
38. **Creators disclose at the start of the caption, out loud in the first seconds, and with the platform's toggle.** The caption starts with "#ad" or "Paid partnership with Cartworth", and the platform's paid-partnership tool is switched on. A free Pro subscription counts as payment.
39. **Creators follow rules A to D, and send their evidence before the post goes live.** A creator's price claim is legally Cartworth's claim. Review every creator post when it's published, and pause payment until any problem is fixed.
40. **Never buy followers, views, likes, saves or reviews, and never give Pro in exchange for a rating or a positive post.** This covers fake or "friend" accounts posing as customers too (FTC Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule).

### G. Platform mechanics

41. **Run business accounts. Use only the TikTok Commercial Music Library, the Meta Sound Collection, or audio you own or have licensed.** No trending songs.
42. **On TikTok, turn on the "Disclose commercial content" toggle** with "Your brand" selected for posts that promote the app.
43. **Don't run paid ads that name a retailer until a lawyer has seen the evidence file format** (new). TikTok's ad policy asks for evidence behind comparative claims.
44. **Get a lawyer to review the official rules before any giveaway.** Every giveaway also needs Instagram's required release and a no-purchase-necessary route.
45. **Read each Reddit and Facebook community's rules before posting there.** Never post the brand account into groups that ban promotion.

### H. When something goes wrong

46. **Follow the correction policy in Part 4.** Never silently delete or quietly edit a number.
47. **Anything that looks like a legal demand from a retailer goes straight to the owner and a lawyer.** That means cease-and-desist letters, lawyer emails and NAD notices. Don't reply publicly or argue in comments. Take the specific post down within 2 hours while it's reviewed.

### Pre-publish check (every post that shows a price or names a store)

- [ ] Evidence file saved, and each named price re-checked within 24h
- [ ] Basis, date and place burned into the image or video
- [ ] Same basis, same product (or the match stated), same week, nearby stores
- [ ] Both prices shown, percentage worked from the higher one, scope words present
- [ ] No superlative outside a stated scope; the sample is named on any aggregate
- [ ] Cheapest number double-checked; no `est` or flagged prices
- [ ] No logos, flyer art, source images or identifiable people
- [ ] No motive words, no savings promises, no "official"
- [ ] Disclaimer and methodology link present
- [ ] Read it aloud as if to the named store's lawyer. Would they have a fair point?

## Part 2. What you need to do

**Before the first post that names a retailer:**

1. **Book an Illinois advertising and IP lawyer.** Bring this document and the Part 5 questions.
2. **Ask the lawyer (and an accountant) whether to form an LLC before launch.** As a sole proprietor, a false-advertising or contract claim is a claim against you personally.
3. **Get a broker's quote for media liability or errors-and-omissions insurance** that covers advertising injury, trade libel and IP claims. Check what it excludes, especially claims about how the data was collected.
4. **Publish two pages on cartworth.app:** a methodology page and a corrections log. Put the link in every bio.
5. **Set up the evidence folder:** one folder per post, with a fixed template for the rule 1 fields.
6. **Settle the "shelf" labels.** In the 21 Sep run (captured 22 Sep), every ALDI shop Cartworth labels "shelf" carries Instacart's own note "Prices vary from in-store" (finding 3.3). Check two or three ALDI and "No markups" stores against real receipts. Bring the result to whoever owns the Cartworth code, because the app shows the same labels. I did not change any code.
7. **Park or rewrite Pillar 3 of the brief.** "Cheapest state", "five cheapest states" and "cheapest meal in America" are all ruled out for now. Suggested replacement: "Milk: what we found at 12 places in 6 states this week", with the sample on screen and shelf and online prices never mixed.
8. **Write down what you've already agreed to.** Note whether you have an Instacart customer account (and since when), and read the Kroger developer terms you accepted when you registered for the Kroger API. Clicking "I agree" can bind you in a way that merely visiting a site may not (finding 3.4).
9. **Get a creator contract template from the lawyer** covering disclosure, claim rules, evidence, usage rights and takedowns before any creator outreach.
10. **Set up the accounts to match the rules:** business accounts, the licensed music libraries, and the bio disclaimer.

**Low-risk defaults until the lawyer weighs in:**

- **Name stores only in single-item, same-basis, dated comparisons built on your own receipts or shelf observations.**
- **Use "Store A / Store B" in app demos** that show the mechanics (unit maths, fine print), not store rankings.
- **Keep posts about weekly-ad fine print on facts.** Restate the terms as text with the ad dates, and never use the ad image.
- **No paid ads, basket comparisons, state rankings or anything about "Instacart markups".**

## Part 3. Findings

### 3.1 Comparative price claims that name competitors

**The baseline is permissive.** The FTC's policy statement on comparative advertising (16 CFR 14.15) says the Commission "encourages the naming of, or reference to competitors, but requires clarity, and, if necessary, disclosure to avoid deception of the consumer." It judges comparative ads by the same standard as other ads, and it disapproves of industry codes that demand *more* substantiation for comparisons than for other claims.

**But every objective claim needs a "reasonable basis" *before* it's made.** Under the FTC's Advertising Substantiation Policy Statement, what counts as reasonable depends on the kind of claim, the harm a false claim could do, and how easy the evidence is to get. A grocery price is easy to check, so a checked price is the expected level of support. If a post says "we checked" or "our data shows", that's an *establishment claim*: you must have actually done the checking you describe.

**Who can come after a bad comparison:**

| Route | What it needs | Why it matters to Cartworth |
|---|---|---|
| **Lanham Act §43(a)(1)(B)** (15 U.S.C. §1125(a)) | A false or misleading statement in "commercial advertising or promotion" about "his or her or another person's goods, services, or commercial activities" that's material and causes injury | A named retailer is "another person". Since *Lexmark v. Static Control* (2014), the plaintiff doesn't need to be a competitor, only to show harm to its sales or business reputation caused by the ad. In many courts, a *literally false* statement (for example, an Instacart price called a shelf price) is presumed to deceive, while a *misleading-but-true* one needs consumer evidence. |
| **Illinois Uniform Deceptive Trade Practices Act,** 815 ILCS 510/2(a)(8) | Disparaging "the goods, services, or business of another by false or misleading representation of fact" | Competition and actual confusion don't have to be proved. Remedies are mainly injunctions. This is home-state law for you. |
| **NAD** (BBB National Programs) | Any company can challenge national advertising | A fast, cheap route for retailers (from $10,500 for a non-member on the standard track in 2026). Decisions come within about 20 business days. If you refuse to take part or to comply, NAD refers the matter to the FTC, and NAD also refers to social platforms such as Meta, which can lead to takedowns. |
| **FTC / Illinois Attorney General** | Deceiving consumers (FTC Act §5; Illinois Consumer Fraud Act) | Less likely for a small account, but it becomes realistic if a post spreads widely. |

**Illinois and FTC price-comparison rules as a benchmark.** Illinois' retail advertising rules (14 Ill. Adm. Code 470.260) let a *seller* compare its price with another seller's price for an identical product only if the comparison price reflects what that seller actually charges now in the trade area. The FTC's pricing guides (16 CFR 233.2) say comparison prices must reflect the prevailing price, not an isolated one. Cartworth doesn't sell groceries, so these probably don't apply directly (a question for the lawyer). They do show how regulators think: identical product, current, local, actual.

**Is "useful content" still advertising?** Probably yes. Under *Bolger v. Youngs Drug Products* (1983), speech counts as commercial when it combines an advertising format, a reference to a specific product and an economic motive. A brand account for a paid app whose data posts point back to that app fits all three. Don't count on "it's just data journalism" as a defence without a lawyer's view.

**What substantiation means for "X is 30% cheaper at Store A than Store B".** The post makes one explicit claim and several implied ones. The evidence must cover each implied claim, or the wording must rule it out:

| What a viewer reasonably takes away | Evidence needed, or wording that rules it out |
|---|---|
| A's price is 30% below B's | Two captures, the arithmetic, both prices shown |
| That's what I'd pay walking in | The same basis at both stores, labelled on the image |
| Right now | Both captured the same day and re-checked within 24h; the date on the image |
| At the A and B near me | Named store locations or city, and a "your stores will differ" line |
| The same product | A like-for-like match, or the match stated on the image |
| It's typical, not a one-off | Sale status and end date shown; the regular price if one side is on sale |
| So A is the cheaper store | **Can't be supported by one item.** Rule it out with scope words. |

A template that meets the rules (fill in from your own receipts):

> **Store-brand whole milk, 1 gallon. Shelf prices from our receipts, Tue 23 Sep 2026, two Chicago stores 1.4 and 2.1 miles from 60618.**
> [Store A]: $[a] (sale, regular $[r], ends [date]) · [Store B]: $[b] (regular)
> A's sale price was [p]% less than B's regular price on this item that day. One item, one day. Your stores and weeks will differ.
> Independent. Not affiliated with either store. How we check: cartworth.app/method

**How a true snapshot becomes a misleading claim.** Each of these can turn accurate numbers into a false net impression:

1. **Different bases.** An Instacart listing against a shelf price.
2. **Different products.** A store brand against a name brand, organic against conventional, or a size difference hidden by rounding. In 2017, NAD recommended that Aldi stop its "save up to 50%" comparisons against H-E-B, Kroger and Walmart. The disclosures were vague, the items chosen weren't shown to be representative, and the comparison matched Aldi's own brand against competitors' name brands. NAD also took jurisdiction partly *because the ads ran on YouTube*, so social posts are within its reach.
3. **Sale against regular.** In *U-Haul v. Jartran* (9th Cir. 1986), Jartran compared its temporary introductory price with U-Haul's regular price, and the result was a $40 million judgment.
4. **Undisclosed conditions.** Loyalty, coupon and multi-buy prices.
5. **Distance.** Stores no one would choose between.
6. **Time.** Captures from different days, or a post re-shared after the prices changed.
7. **Item to store.** "Aldi is cheaper."
8. **Sample to population.** "Cheapest state."
9. **Cherry-picking.** One favourable item implied to be typical.
10. **Absolutes.** "Always", "cheapest", "lowest".
11. **Framing.** Truncated chart axes, red for the "expensive" store, "rip-off" sound effects or captions. The FTC and courts judge the overall impression, including visuals and audio.

**Worked examples from Cartworth's own capture (stats week of 21 Sep 2026).** These are *illustrations of what the rules catch*. They are not verified and must not be posted.

- *"Milk is almost 3x the price at Jewel-Osco as ALDI."* In the 22 Sep capture for Chicago, Cartworth picked ALDI Friendly Farms whole milk at $2.42 and Jewel-Osco Kemps whole milk at $6.99. That post would break five rules:
  - ALDI's figure is a **sale** price (regular $3.06).
  - It's labelled "shelf", but that ALDI shop carries Instacart's note "Prices vary from in-store".
  - Jewel's figure is an **Instacart listing**, so the bases differ.
  - Kemps is a **name brand** against ALDI's store brand, while the same capture also lists a "Jewel Milk" gallon at $3.99.
  - The picked products weren't checked by a person.

  If that $3.99 listing is whole milk, an honest store-brand comparison might come to about 1.6x, or roughly 1.3x on regular prices, and it still couldn't be posted because the bases differ.
- *"Milk costs 3.5x more at Safeway than ALDI."* The capture has ALDI Peoria at $2.28 ("shelf") and Safeway San Francisco at $7.99 (an Instacart listing). The stores are 2,000 miles apart on different bases. It fails rules 12 and 16 on sight.
- *"Cheapest eggs in the country: Kroger, Peoria, $1.19."* That number is an **Instacart listing**. "Country" means 12 ZIPs in 6 states.
- *"Illinois has the cheapest milk; Montana the priciest."* The capture covers 6 states and 2 metros each, and the pipeline itself marks every item `confidence: low`. Walmart and ALDI lookups both failed in Billings and Missoula, so Montana's rank may reflect which stores could be reached, not what things cost.
- *"ALDI is the cheapest chain for milk (4 of 5 states)."* This compares ALDI's "shelf" labels with other chains' Instacart listings, and it turns an item result into a claim about the whole store.

### 3.2 Retailer names, logos, store photos, packaging

**Names in text are the low-risk part.** Nominative fair use allows a mark to be used to refer to its owner when three conditions are met: it's needed to identify the store, you use only as much as you need, and nothing suggests sponsorship. That's the *New Kids* test, applied in *Toyota v. Tabari* (9th Cir. 2010). Congress wrote comparative advertising into the fair-use exclusion from *dilution* claims (15 U.S.C. §1125(c)(3)(A)). The Seventh Circuit, which covers Illinois, hasn't formally adopted a separate nominative fair-use test. It has acknowledged the underlying principle in comparative advertising and otherwise uses the ordinary likelihood-of-confusion analysis, which is a point for the lawyer.

**Logos are where it goes wrong.**

- **Tabari:** the court said the stylized Lexus mark and "L" logo the brokers had used could lead consumers to think they were an authorised Toyota affiliate. By trial they had removed both and added a disclaimer.
- **Skiplagged (N.D. Tex., Oct. 2024):** the jury found the travel site's use of American Airlines' marks was nominative fair use, then awarded $9.4 million for copyright infringement of the airline's logo. The verdict may be subject to post-trial motions or appeal.
- **Jack Daniel's v. VIP (2023):** using someone else's mark as *your own* source identifier gets no special protection. That's why rule 27 keeps retailer names out of handles, series titles and brand hashtags.

**Does the App Store conclusion carry over to social? Yes, and more strongly.** The App Store work found that the real exposure was the imagery the app pulls in: flyer clippings with retailer marks and ad typography (`marketing/screenshots/README.md`, shot #3). On social:

- **Images are the whole medium.** The brief's own advice is "the screen recording is the content", and every recording shows product thumbnails and ad images pulled from Instacart and Flipp.
- **Product photos have their own copyright.** Someone else's product photograph is a copyrighted work even if the product in it isn't (*Ets-Hokin v. Skyy Spirits*, 9th Cir. 2000). In the app, those images appear inside search results. Put into a Cartworth post, they become Cartworth copying them for its own advertising.
- **Store-brand packaging is the retailer's own trademark.** A hero shot of an ALDI-brand jug is retailer branding in your ad.

**Store photography.**

- Exteriors seen from a public place are fine as far as the *building's* copyright goes (17 U.S.C. §120(a)), but the sign on the building is a logo.
- Inside a store, the owner sets the rules and can ask you to leave (ACLU guidance for photographers).
- Anyone identifiable in a promotional post triggers the Illinois Right of Publicity Act, which requires prior *written* consent to use a person's identity for advertising (765 ILCS 1075/30).
- Secretly recording private conversations, for example staff talking at a shelf, is eavesdropping under 720 ILCS 5/14-2.

**Net:** text and your own charts carry almost no risk. Your own photos of products you bought carry little. In-store footage and images taken from sources carry the risk.

### 3.3 Price basis disclosure

**What Instacart itself says.** Instacart's Help Center says retailers set their Instacart prices, some set them differently from in-store, and some in-store sales don't apply. Each storefront carries its own pricing note. Instacart has also said it's pushing retailers toward matching in-store prices, including pilots in Chicago (December 2025). So a store's basis can change, and the note has to be read again at each capture.

**Instacart prices have been a live consumer-protection issue.** A late-2025 study by Groundwork Collaborative, Consumer Reports and More Perfect Union found that different shoppers saw different prices for the same item, at the same store, at the same time. The average gap was about 7%, and some were as high as 23%. The FTC reportedly opened an inquiry. On 22 December 2025, Instacart ended all item price tests and said shoppers at the same store at the same time now see the same prices. Two implications:

- Instacart prices from before that date are shakier evidence.
- Posts about Instacart pricing fall on a subject that's already heated, involving a well-funded company that has publicly defended itself.

**What disclosure law requires.** No statute I found requires a "basis" label as such. The general rule (FTC Act §5, the Lanham Act and Illinois law) is that the overall impression must be true. The FTC's *.com Disclosures* guidance adds three points:

- A disclosure can qualify a claim but can't cure a false one. If it contradicts the claim, the claim itself has to change.
- The disclosure must sit as close to the claim as possible and be hard to miss.
- In video, it has to be made in the same way the claim is (on screen, and out loud if the claim is spoken).

A viewer who sees "Store A $2.42 · Store B $3.99" assumes both are what they'd pay at the register. If the gap depends partly on B being an Instacart listing, a footnote doesn't fix that. That's why rule 12 bans cross-basis comparisons outright, and rule 2 requires the label on the image.

**A problem with Cartworth's own labels (flag for the owner and for whoever owns the code).**

- **The labelling rule.** `src/adapters/instacart.js` (line 448) labels a price "shelf" when the Instacart shop's service type is `instore` or its price note matches "no markup".
- **ALDI.** In `stats/samples.jsonl` (run of 21 Sep, captured 22 Sep), all nine ALDI shops that returned prices are `instore` and labelled "shelf", yet each carries Instacart's own price note "Prices vary from in-store".
- **Stores labelled by note.** Walmart, Hy-Vee, Lucky and Key Food are labelled "shelf" because the note says "No markups". That's the retailer's own label on an online listing, not a checked shelf price.

Either may be accurate, but nothing in the data proves it. On the owner's rule of never implying more precision than the source supports, a public post shouldn't call these "shelf" until a receipt confirms it (rule 9). ALDI is the chain the captured data most often shows as cheapest, so this is also where a mislabel would do the most damage. I did not edit any code.

**The legal landscape, stated factually and without overstating either way:**

- **Prices are facts, and facts aren't copyrightable** (*Feist v. Rural Telephone*, 1991). The photos, descriptions and flyer artwork around them are. Copyright is weakest for a bare price and strongest for images, hence rule 28.
- **Contract is the main lever, and whether the terms bind you turns on consent.**
  - In *hiQ v. LinkedIn*, hiQ was found to have breached LinkedIn's user agreement, which it had accepted and which its fake profiles violated. The case ended in December 2022 with a $500,000 judgment and an injunction.
  - In *Meta v. Bright Data* (N.D. Cal., January 2024), the court held that Meta's terms didn't forbid scraping public data while logged out. Meta then dropped the case.
- **The closest analogy to publishing prices.** In *Southwest Airlines v. Kiwi.com* (N.D. Tex., September 2021), the court found Kiwi had agreed to Southwest's terms, including by buying tickets on Southwest's site. It barred Kiwi from scraping *and from publishing Southwest's flight and fare information*, and that became a permanent injunction when the case settled. An important difference: Kiwi also resold Southwest tickets with added fees, which Cartworth doesn't do.
- **I found no US case on republishing grocery shelf prices** in social posts or comparisons, either way. This is an open question.

**Why social raises the stakes compared with the app:**

- **It's Cartworth's own advertising,** not a tool that shoppers run on their own phones.
- **Every post is permanent, public evidence** of which data you used and how you used it.
- **Republishing is named outright.** Flipp's terms specifically ban republishing or posting its content on any internet site.
- **Naming a retailer invites it to look at how the data was collected,** and Instacart's "competing product" clause is written broadly.

That's why rule 34 keeps comparisons that name a retailer on first-hand data until a lawyer has looked at it.

**FTC Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255, revised 29 June 2023). They apply the moment Cartworth pays a creator or gives one Pro:**

- **Free Pro counts as payment.** Free or discounted products are a "material connection" that has to be disclosed, just like cash.
- **Disclosures have to be impossible to miss.** "Clear and conspicuous" now means difficult to miss and easily understood, and on social media it must be *unavoidable*. Nobody should have to click "more" to see it.
- **The platform's label isn't enough on its own.** The FTC says responsibility sits with the brand and the creator, not the platform.
- **Disclose in the video itself.** Say it on screen, and out loud if the endorsement is spoken. A caption alone isn't enough.
- **Cartworth has to train and monitor creators.** It must tell them what they can and can't say, check what they post, and act on problems.
- **Creators can't make claims Cartworth couldn't back up, and Cartworth answers for them.** If a creator says "Aldi is 40% cheaper than Jewel", Cartworth needs the evidence.
- **The same goes for the owner and staff.** Anyone connected to Cartworth has to disclose that connection every time they mention it, including on forums and review sites. A disclosure on a profile page doesn't count.

**FTC Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule (16 CFR Part 465, in force since 21 Oct 2024):**

- **Fake reviews and paid sentiment are banned.** No fake or AI-generated reviews, and no paying for reviews conditioned on what they say.
- **Undisclosed insider reviews are banned.**
- **Buying fake followers, views or other influence metrics is banned** when you know, or should know, that they're fake and use them for commercial purposes.
- **It can mean civil penalties for knowing violations.** The reported 2026 maximum is $53,088 per violation.

### 3.6 Reputation: who pushes back, and how

- **Retailers.** The likely order of escalation:
  - a comment or DM from the retailer's social team
  - a lawyer's letter demanding removal
  - an NAD challenge, which is fast and can end in a platform referral
  - a Lanham Act or Illinois UDTPA suit, which is rare against a small account but more likely if a post spreads or keeps repeating after a complaint

  A consistent, published correction record is the best evidence of good faith at every one of those stages.
- **Fans.** ALDI, Trader Joe's, Costco, H-E-B, Publix and Wegmans all have passionate fan bases. A comparison that makes a loved store look expensive draws pile-ons. One that keeps flattering the same chain looks paid-for.
  - Rotate stores and items.
  - Publish win/loss counts.
  - Answer "who pays you?" with the plain fact: no store does.
- **Screenshots outlive deletions.** That's the reason for rule 3: the basis, date and place travel inside the image. A corrected post is only as good as its correction's reach, hence Part 4.
- **Instacart as a topic.** Grocery pricing stories move fast. The late-2025 price-test study went from report to a reported FTC inquiry to Instacart ending the practice within weeks. A Cartworth post framed as an exposé would land on the same nerve. Keep to "[Store]'s Instacart listing" and stick to the facts.
- **The owner's rule.** A wrong price in a post names a retailer publicly, and screenshots of it last forever. So an unverifiable price is treated as a wrong one.

## Part 4. Correction policy (draft, suitable to publish at cartworth.app/corrections)

> **Cartworth corrections policy**
>
> We publish grocery prices, and a wrong price is the worst mistake we can make. When we get one wrong, we fix it quickly and where you'll see it.
>
> **What we correct:** a wrong price, size or product; the wrong store, place or date; a price labelled with the wrong source (for example, an online listing called a shelf price); a maths error; and anything worded so that a reasonable reader would take away something untrue about a store.
>
> **How to report:** reply to the post, message us, or email support@cartworth.app with the store, the item and when you saw the price. A receipt or photo helps but isn't required.
>
> **What we do:**
> 1. We reply publicly within 4 hours during the day to say we're checking.
> 2. We verify within 24 hours. **If we can't confirm the price, we treat it as wrong.**
> 3. If it's wrong, we post a correction in the same format as the original (a video for a video, an image for an image). We pin it for 7 days and link it in a reply to the original post and to the person who reported it.
> 4. Where the platform allows editing, we edit the caption to begin "CORRECTED [date]: …".
> 5. Where it doesn't (most images and videos), we take the original down *after* the correction is up, and the correction says we did.
> 6. We add every correction to the log on this page: the date, the post, what was wrong, what's right, and what we changed so it doesn't happen again.
>
> **What we never do:** quietly delete or edit a price, argue with the person who reported it, or delete good-faith criticism.
>
> **Stores:** if a store tells us a price is wrong and gives details we can check, we correct it on the same terms. We're independent and aren't affiliated with or paid by any store.

**Internal steps that go with it (not published):**

- **Stop the post.** Pause scheduled posts that use the same data, and any boosts, until the cause is known.
- **Trace every error to its root cause.** Check whether the same fault affects the app or the website. If it does, fix it in both implementations in the same change (the owner's standing rule).
- **Creator errors:** ask for an edit or repost within 24 hours, and hold payment until it's fixed.
- **Retailer legal demands follow rule 47:** take the post down while it's reviewed, involve the lawyer, and make no public argument.

## Part 5. Questions for a lawyer

1. **Are Cartworth's price posts "commercial advertising or promotion"?** Answer this for the Lanham Act and for Illinois law, including the data posts that promote nothing directly.
2. **What substantiation file is enough** for a single-item comparison that names a store, how long should it be kept, and does the Part 1 template cover it?
3. **Which of these terms bind me, and would restating a few prices in a post breach them?**
   - Instacart's terms, which claim to bind anyone who *visits*, and possibly my own Instacart customer account
   - ALDI's corporate terms (Illinois law, arbitration in Cook County)
   - Flipp's app terms
   - the Kroger developer terms I accepted

   Could a *Southwest v. Kiwi*-style injunction reach the app as well as the posts?
4. **Does Instacart's "competing product" clause reach Cartworth?** Would an authorised interface, such as any developer programme Instacart offers (not checked here), or a licence change the analysis?
5. **Should Cartworth be an LLC before launch,** and what insurance (media liability or E&O, with advertising injury and IP cover) is worth buying? Check the exclusions for how data was collected.
6. **How does the Seventh Circuit treat nominative use** of retailer names in social posts and hashtags?
7. **Is showing third-party product thumbnails in screen recordings of the app acceptable** (fair use or incidental), or should they always be blurred?
8. **Would calling Instacart-derived prices "shelf"** (ALDI "in-store" shops, "No markups" stores) be defensible if challenged?
9. **What should the creator agreement template say?** Cover disclosure, claim rules, evidence, usage rights, takedown and payment holds.
10. **What's the response plan for a cease-and-desist or an NAD challenge?** Should Cartworth take part in NAD, and at what cost?
11. **Do Illinois Part 470 or the FTC pricing guides apply to a comparison publisher that doesn't sell groceries?**
12. **What do the official rules need to say if Cartworth ever runs a giveaway?**
